\title{What Limits Vowel-Length Restoration in Old English?}
\author{Mahbod Tajdini \and Hanna Miranovich \and Arjun Singh Bhadoria\\[2pt]
Essentials in Text and Speech Processing, University of Zurich, HS 2026}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Editors of Old English add macrons for long vowels that the manuscripts do not write. We ask what limits automatic
restoration of these marks and measure four levers on the same held-out tokens: dictionary coverage, sentence
context, editorial convention and disagreement between editors. Coverage moves the most: a frequency prior on
Tauber's \textit{Beowulf} rises from \NTBeoAllFreq\% to \NABeoAllFreq\% of tokens when dictionaries supply the
candidates. Context helps where grammar decides the reading. On grammatical homographs in Alfred's
\textit{Soliloquies}, a ByT5 scorer that sees two words on either side reaches \NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwo\%, against
\NEncCapfullSolClsBWord\% for the word alone, but it gains nothing on lexical or editorial homographs, and on
\textit{Beowulf} the word alone does as well as any context. The Harrison and Sharp and Wyatt editions of
\textit{Beowulf} agree on \NPairHWLex\% of ambiguous tokens and on only \NPairHWClsB\% of grammatical ones, so part of what remains is
editorial convention that no model can read from the text.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Old English manuscripts do not mark vowel length. Modern editions add macrons, and the marks separate words that are
otherwise spelled alike: \emph{god} `God' and \emph{gōd} `good', the relative particle \emph{þe} and the pronoun
\emph{þē} `thee'. Restoring them means choosing among candidate length patterns for each bare word. Readers, metrical
analysis and grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) tools need the marks, but Old English NLP corpora lack them.

We ask what limits this restoration and measure four levers on the same tokens: whether the right candidate exists
(coverage), whether neighbouring words decide among candidates (context), whether training and test editions mark a
word alike (editorial convention), and how far two editors agree (the ceiling). We contribute a cleaned, length-marked
corpus of \NCorpusTokens{} tokens from nine editions % data/manifest.json
with work-level splits and a leakage audit, an editor-agreement measurement for \textit{Beowulf}, a controlled
comparison of ByT5 context windows, a coverage ablation and an error analysis of our model and of CharsiuG2P.

\paragraph{Related work.} Yarowsky \cite{yarowsky1994} restored Spanish and French accents with decision lists over
local context. For Latin, Winge \cite{winge2015} combines a tagger, a lemmatiser and a morphological analyser and
agrees with a human annotator on about 98\% of vowel lengths. Thörn Cleland and Cullhed \cite{cleland2026} propagate
Ancient Greek length marks across the forms of a lexeme and train a small character transformer on the result.
Esmail et al.\ \cite{esmail2022} measure how much lookahead matters for Arabic diacritisation, the question our
left-context arm asks for Old English. CharsiuG2P \cite{zhu2022} covers Old English in a 100-language ByT5 G2P model whose Old English data come
from WikiPron \cite{lee2020}, but it reads isolated words. Tauber \cite{tauber2021} aligned \textit{Beowulf} token by token with
length marks, which gives us a gold standard. We know of no evaluation on Old English running text that sets a model
against the disagreement between editors.

\section{Candidates, populations and ambiguity classes}
A word's pattern has one 0/1 slot per vowel letter, and a candidate source proposes the possible patterns for each
bare word. Our headline source is the training forms plus WikiPron \cite{lee2020}, whose
Old English IPA we convert to spellings by aligning vowels (\emph{ea, eo, ie, io} count as one) with IPA nuclei (\NWikiForms{} forms from
\NWikiRows{} rows, \NWikiAligned\% aligned). The proposal named three dictionaries: Clark Hall in TEI
\cite{clarkhall}, Wiktionary inflection tables via kaikki.org \cite{ylonen2022} and Bosworth-Toller \cite{bosworth}.
We rebuilt all three after fixing three conversion bugs. The frequency prior picks a word's most frequent training
pattern and, for an unseen word, the pattern most dictionaries attest, then the one with fewest long vowels.

We score four populations. A token is \emph{lexicon-ambiguous} when its word has at least two candidates
(\NWSolLexN{} of \NWSolAllN{} prose and \NWBeoLexN{} of \NWBeoAllN{} verse tokens), \emph{attested-ambiguous} when two
patterns each occur at least twice in training, and a \emph{minority reading} when its gold pattern occurs in training
but is not the majority. The 60 % data/ambiguity_classes.json, n_skeletons
most frequent ambiguous skeletons also carry a class: \NClassA{} \emph{lexical} (A,
two words: \emph{god/gōd}), \NClassB{} \emph{grammatical} (B, closed-class words of different function:
\emph{þe/þē}) and \NClassC{} \emph{editorial} (C, one word marked by habit: \emph{se/sē}). Two independent LLM-based
annotators assigned the classes (Cohen's $\kappa=\NClassKappa$ \cite{cohen1960}), an automated adjudicator settled
their two disagreements, % data/ambiguity_classes.json, n_disagreements
and no team member has reviewed the labels yet.

\section{Systems and protocol}\label{sec:systems}
All systems choose among the same candidates and predict all-short for a word without any. Besides the prior,
logistic classifiers (scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011}, log loss) see the word with its first and last two letters
(\emph{word}), plus the two preceding words (\emph{left}), plus the two following words (\emph{bilateral}). Our main
model fine-tunes ByT5-small \cite{xue2022} with Transformers \cite{wolf2020} as a candidate scorer. The input is the
word alone or its context with the target marked \texttt{[[target]]}, the output is the pattern string (e.g.\
\texttt{01}), and at test time each candidate is scored by the summed log-probability of its string. It trains on the
\NEncTrainExamples{} lexicon-ambiguous training tokens. Arms differ only in the input: \emph{word}, \emph{left} (all
preceding words), \emph{sentence} (both sides), \emph{local2} and \emph{local5} (two or five words on each side), cut
at 128, 256 % results/encoder/summary.json, settings.max_bytes
or \NEncFullCap{} bytes (full). Local inputs never exceed 103 bytes, % results/encoder/summary.json, local_replicates.note
so their runs at the three caps share inputs and we pool them.

We chose the training setting on the development set only, with \NSelRuns{} sentence-arm runs that crossed learning
rate, epochs, Zupitza in or out, and two seeds. The best mean ambiguous-token accuracy came from learning rate
$3\cdot10^{-4}$, % results/encoder/selection.json, choice
\NSelEpochs{} epochs and no Zupitza (\NSelDevNoZup\%, against \NSelDevZup\% at best with it). Dropping Zupitza also
helps the prior (\NDevFreqZup\% to \NDevFreqNoZup\%) and the bilateral model (\NDevBilZup\% to \NDevBilNoZup\%) on
development ambiguous tokens, so no final system trains on it. The price is coverage: the prior loses
\NDThreeSolAllCost{} points on all prose test tokens. Three seeds per arm and cap, with the word arm run at the full cap only, make \NGridRuns{} runs.

We report means and standard deviations over seeds, and differences with a 95\% interval from a paired bootstrap
\cite{efron1993} that resamples whole sentences 1,000 times, % src/oevl/stats.py
using each token's correctness averaged over seeds. We do not rely on single-seed McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947},
whose outcome flipped when one training token changed.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Editor agreement and the gate}\label{sec:editors}
Table~\ref{tab:editors} compares the three \textit{Beowulf} editions on the tokens each pair aligns. Harrison \& Sharp
and Wyatt agree on \NPairHWLex\% of lexicon-ambiguous tokens, and Wyatt and Tauber almost exactly as often. Editors
part on class B: Wyatt agrees with the other two on only \NPairHWClsB\% and \NPairWTClsB\% of grammatical homographs,
mostly over \emph{ne/nē} and \emph{þe/þē}. % results/agreement/disagreements.tsv
Harrison \& Sharp and Tauber agree far more closely, and the source likely explains part of it. Our Harrison \& Sharp file is
the Project Gutenberg revision of their fourth edition, whose producer changed a listed set of quantities to modern
values, among them \emph{ne} to \emph{nē} as a conjunction and \emph{se} to \emph{sē} as a relative \cite{editions}.
That pair is not two independent nineteenth-century opinions, so we take Harrison \& Sharp against Wyatt as the
two-editor reference.

An LLM-based assistant labelled a random \NCheckN{} of the Harrison \& Sharp and Wyatt disagreements as
\NCheckEditorial{} editorial differences, \NCheckNorm{} macrons on the other letter of a digraph, \NCheckAlign{}
alignment faults and \NCheckOther{} others, mostly probable slips. No team member has checked these labels.

Our plan foresaw a gate before modelling but fixed its rule only after the agreement numbers were in: continue if
editor agreement beats the prior by at least 10 points on ambiguous tokens. % results/agreement/metrics.json, gate.rule
The gate therefore decided nothing. Only Harrison \& Sharp against Tauber
clears it (\NGateHTHs{} points over the Harrison \& Sharp floor), the pair the revision likely brought closer. The other gaps
lie between \NGateHWWyatt{} and \NGateHTTauber{} points. Scored against Harrison \& Sharp or Wyatt, the prior is
only \NGateHWWyatt{} to \NGateHWHs{} points below the level at which these two editors agree.

\subsection{Context}
Table~\ref{tab:encoder} gives the ByT5 grid. On prose every context arm beats the word arm and the prior on
lexicon-ambiguous tokens: the full-cap sentence arm by $\NEncCapfullSolLexBilVsWord$ points over word
\iv{\NEncCapfullSolLexBilVsWordLo}{\NEncCapfullSolLexBilVsWordHi}, pooled local2 by $\NEncRepSolLexLocalTwoVsWord$
\iv{\NEncRepSolLexLocalTwoVsWordLo}{\NEncRepSolLexLocalTwoVsWordHi}. Almost all of it comes from class B, where local2
beats word by $\NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwoVsWord$ points
\iv{\NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwoVsWordLo}{\NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwoVsWordHi}. Classes A and C do not move: the prior and
every full-cap arm score \NEncCapfullSolClsAFreq\% on class A, and class C stays between \NEncCapfullSolClsCFreq\% and

On verse, context still helps class B (local2 beats word by $\NEncRepBeoClsBLocalTwoVsWord$ points
\iv{\NEncRepBeoClsBLocalTwoVsWordLo}{\NEncRepBeoClsBLocalTwoVsWordHi}), but on all lexicon-ambiguous tokens the word
arm (\NEncCapfullBeoLexWord\%) is as good as any context arm. The full-cap sentence arm is slightly worse
($\NEncCapfullBeoLexBilVsWord$ \iv{\NEncCapfullBeoLexBilVsWordLo}{\NEncCapfullBeoLexBilVsWordHi}), and the best
context number, left at 128 bytes (\NEncCapOneTwoEightBeoLexLeft\%), ties with it. We cannot show any effect of the
byte cap, and two words on each side match the full sentence. Why verse differs we do not know. Its freer word order
is one candidate that a breakdown by construction could test.

Run-to-run variance limits what the table can say. Local5 runs with identical inputs, data order and seed differ by
up to \NEncRepSolClsBLocalFiveSpread{} points on prose class B (local2: \NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwoSpread). The bootstrap
intervals leave out this training variance, so we do not claim differences between arms below about one point. None
of the \NGridRuns{} grid runs collapsed, but in model selection one \NCollapseEpochs-epoch run nearly always chose the
candidate with most long marks, scoring \NCollapseSolAll\% on all prose tokens and \NCollapseSolLex\% on ambiguous
ones. An earlier exploratory sentence-arm result in our project had the same signature.

The logistic models split the same way with smaller gains. On prose the bilateral model beats the word model by
$\NBootSolLexBilVsWord$ points \iv{\NBootSolLexBilVsWordLo}{\NBootSolLexBilVsWordHi} on ambiguous tokens and by
$\NBootSolClsBBilVsWord$ \iv{\NBootSolClsBBilVsWordLo}{\NBootSolClsBBilVsWordHi} on class B, against
$\NEncRepSolClsBLocalTwoVsWord$ for ByT5 local2. On verse the difference to the word model is $\NBootBeoLexBilVsWord$
\iv{\NBootBeoLexBilVsWordLo}{\NBootBeoLexBilVsWordHi}.

\subsection{Coverage}
Table~\ref{tab:coverage} runs the prior, the bilateral model and the oracle (the gold pattern whenever it is a
candidate) with four candidate sources. With training forms alone, \NBeoUnknown\% of \textit{Beowulf} tokens are

\subsection{CharsiuG2P}
We reproduce CharsiuG2P's published Old English scores exactly (WER \NCharsiuWer, PER \NCharsiuPer). Its evaluation % results/charsiu/benchmark.json, max_length_20 and max_length_50
stops decoding after 20 steps and so cuts \NCharsiuTrunc{} of its \NCharsiuN{} references short. With 50 steps the WER
is \NCharsiuWerFixed, and \NCharsiuLenOnly{} of the \NCharsiuWordErrors{} word errors (\NCharsiuLenShare\%) differ from
the reference only in vowel length. On our test tokens, with its \texttt{<ang>:} prefix and its IPA mapped to length
patterns, it gets length right on \NCharsiuSolAll\% of prose and \NCharsiuBeoAll\% of verse tokens, below our prior
(\NWSolAllFreq\% and \NWBeoAllFreq\%). CharsiuG2P was built for word lists from Wiktionary transcriptions, not to
follow an edition's conventions in running text, so this gap measures a mismatch of task as much as of model. Its
own benchmark confirms the proposal's expectation that a type-level model fails mostly on length.

\section{Error analysis}
Two independent LLM-based annotators typed \NErrItems{} random lexicon-ambiguous errors each of the ByT5 sentence arm
(full cap, seed~1) and of CharsiuG2P ($\kappa=\NErrKappa$). An automated adjudicator resolved their disagreements, and
the labels await review by the team (Table~\ref{tab:errors}). The CharsiuG2P sample predates decision D3. ByT5 errors
spread evenly over homographs (\NErrEncHomograph), grammatical pairs (\NErrEncGrammatical) and editorial habit
(\NErrEncEditorial). For \NErrEncGoldInDoubt{} more, another edition supports the prediction, and for
\NErrEncCeiling{} the gold is not a candidate.

Two examples show both ends. In the \textit{Soliloquies} sentence \emph{Ic clypie tō þē Drihten þū þe æall
geworhtest} `I call to thee, Lord, thou who madest all', the first \emph{þe} is the pronoun and the second the
relative particle. The prior, the logistic models and every word-arm run mark both short, and all
\NToTheEncLocalTwoRuns{} local2 runs get both right. Of the \NToTheN{} long \emph{þē} after \emph{tō} in this text the
word arm finds \NToTheEncWordMax, the local2 runs \NToTheEncLocalTwoMin{} to \NToTheEncLocalTwoMax, and the
sentence-arm runs anywhere from \NToTheEncBilMin{} to \NToTheEncBilMax, the instability of the previous section in one
word. In \textit{Beowulf} line 2796, % data/brunetti-length.txt
\emph{ēcum dryhtne þē ic hēr on starie} `(thanks to) the eternal Lord (for the treasures) that I gaze on here',
Tauber and Harrison \& Sharp print \emph{þē}, Wyatt prints \emph{þe}, and every full-cap ByT5 run chooses \emph{þe}.
The annotators marked this error `gold in doubt': the model agrees with one editor and is scored against another.

\section{Deviations, limitations and conclusion}
\paragraph{Deviations.} The plan described an encoder with a classification head per vowel slot. We built a
sequence-to-sequence scorer instead, which still chooses among dictionary candidates and trains one checkpoint on
identical data per arm. We did not run the planned BiLSTM sanity model. LLM-based annotators, not two team members,
labelled the ambiguity classes, the error types and the disagreement sample. The gate rule was written after the
agreement numbers, Zupitza left training by a development-set decision, and the dataset has a Hugging Face export
script but is not uploaded.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We predict editorial convention, not pronunciation: the dictionaries and the gold descend
from the same nineteenth-century philology. Tauber's gold is a third editorial opinion, and one agreement edition is
partly modernised. Three seeds do not capture the training variance of the context arms, and the 60 classified
skeletons were chosen on a corpus that still included Zupitza.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Coverage is the largest lever: dictionaries add \NBeoGainDict{} points on \textit{Beowulf},
while the best context arm adds \NEncBestCtxGainSol{} points on all prose tokens. Context resolves grammatical
homographs such as \emph{þe/þē}, and two words on each side suffice, but it leaves lexical and editorial ambiguity
untouched. The annotators label \NErrEncEditorial{} of \NErrItems{} sampled errors as editorial habit, and on
grammatical words two editors agree on only \NPairHWClsB\% of tokens, so a single gold edition cannot tell a wrong
model from a different convention. The next step is to train and test within one editorial tradition and to have the
automated labels checked by the team.
